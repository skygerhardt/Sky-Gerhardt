# Perfect Competition — Engagement Brief

## The problem

The farm’s primary decision is how to divide its available land between tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun in a way that generates the most money without using more labor or land than it has available. There are 64 beds, and each crop also has an individual bed limit. The farm has limited labor from the owner and can hire up to four temporary workers. Therefore, the highest-revenue crop is not always the best crop to plant the most of.

## What I am assuming

I am taking the prices, bed limits, labor requirements, fertilizer costs, and diminishing-returns rates provided in the case as given. I am also assuming that the farm can hire up to four temporary workers at the stated wage and that these workers can provide the full amount of labor listed in the case. If I had more information, I would want to test whether market prices and labor availability stay consistent throughout the season, because changes in either could affect the best planting mix.

## Hypothesis

Tomatoes generate $8,800 per bed, which is much higher than carrots or mesclun. At first glance, this makes tomatoes seem like the smartest choice. However, tomatoes also require the most labor per bed and have a 10% diminishing-returns rate. This is much higher than carrots at 2.5% and mesclun at 1.25%. That makes me think planting tomatoes all the way to their 20-bed maximum would become too labor-intensive.

Carrots bring in less revenue, but they require significantly less labor and fertilizer. Mesclun has the lowest diminishing-returns rate, which makes me think it can be expanded further without labor requirements increasing as quickly.

Because of this, I anticipate that the optimal strategy will use all or almost all of the carrot and mesclun capacity while keeping tomatoes below their full 20-bed capacity. I estimate an optimal mix of roughly 12 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. That uses 62 of the 64 available beds and reflects my prediction that labor, rather than just land, will become the primary constraint.

## How I would know I was wrong

If the model indicates that tomatoes should be planted nearly to their full 20-bed capacity, or if the optimal solution substantially reduces carrots or mesclun instead, I would consider my hypothesis incorrect.
